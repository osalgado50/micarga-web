International and multimodal

Air freight: AWB air waybill, chargeable weight and the rules you need to know

Air freight is the fastest option when you need to move an urgent, high-value load or one with a critical delivery date.

But speed comes at a price: more checks, more paperwork and pricing that is very different from road or sea transport.

If you want to avoid delays, extra costs or rejections at the airport, you need to control five things:

  • The AWB air waybill.
  • Chargeable or volumetric weight.
  • IATA agent and cargo security.
  • Dangerous goods and the IATA DGR rules.
  • Coordination between aircraft, road and customs.

Here are the particulars you should know before you book or manage an air shipment.

What is air freight?

Air freight means moving goods by aircraft between two airports, usually with a pickup and a final delivery by road.

It is especially useful for:

  • Urgent industrial components.
  • Pharmaceutical and healthcare products.
  • Critical spare parts.
  • Electronics and high-value goods.
  • International shipments with very tight deadlines.
  • Perishable or temperature-sensitive products.

Its main advantage is clear: it cuts transit times.

So is its main drawback: the cost per kilogram is higher than sea freight and, in many cases, higher than road transport.

That is why you shouldn't always send the whole load by air. Use air freight when the time saved makes up for the price difference.

The AWB air waybill is the central document

The AWB (Air Waybill) is the air consignment note. It evidences the contract of carriage between the shipper and the airline, and confirms that the airline or its agent has received the goods.

It is not a document of title or a negotiable document. Its job is to document the carriage, state the terms of the shipment and let every party work from the same information.

Among other details, the AWB must show:

  • Shipper: who sends the goods.
  • Consignee: who is to receive them.
  • Airport of origin and destination.
  • Description of the cargo.
  • Number of packages.
  • Gross weight and chargeable weight.
  • Type of service and special conditions.
  • Handling instructions.
  • Dangerous goods information, where applicable.

In a consolidated shipment you may come across two documents:

  • MAWB: the master air waybill issued for the airline.
  • HAWB: the air waybill issued by the forwarder for each customer or shipment included in the consolidation.

A mistake in the address, the weight, the description or the paperwork can lead to an extra inspection or to the cargo being refused.

Chargeable weight: you pay for the actual weight or for the space taken up

In air cargo you don't always pay for what the scales say.

The airline compares two values:

1. Gross weight: what the goods actually weigh with their packaging. 2. Volumetric weight: the weight equivalent to the space they take up.

The chargeable weight is whichever of the two is higher.

How to work out volumetric weight

The usual formula for air freight is:

Length × width × height in centimetres ÷ 6,000

For example, for a package measuring:

  • Length: 120 cm.
  • Width: 80 cm.
  • Height: 120 cm.

The calculation would be:

120 × 80 × 120 ÷ 6,000 = 192 kg. If the package actually weighs 150 kg, the chargeable weight will be 192 kg, because it takes up more space than its physical weight reflects.

If the actual weight were 220 kg, you would pay on 220 kg.

This rule stops a very light but bulky load from taking up a large share of the aircraft's capacity without it showing in the price.

What to check before asking for a quote

Before requesting a quote, have ready:

  • The outside dimensions of each package.
  • The gross weight of each unit.
  • The total number of packages.
  • The type of packaging.
  • Whether it can be stacked.
  • Any temperature or handling requirements.
  • Airport of origin and destination.

Don't ask for a price with just the actual kilograms. If you don't give the dimensions, the quote may change once the airline works out the chargeable weight.

IATA agent: more control and fewer mistakes

An IATA agent is a forwarder or intermediary accredited to handle air cargo operations with airlines under the industry's applicable procedures.

Their work may include:

  • Preparing or issuing the transport documents.
  • Booking space on flights.
  • Working out the chargeable weight.
  • Coordinating the consolidation of goods.
  • Checking security requirements.
  • Handling dangerous goods paperwork.
  • Coordinating customs, handling and road delivery.

IATA does not replace the aviation authorities. Its regulations and standards apply alongside the relevant international, European and national rules.

When you work with an agent, ask them to confirm in writing:

  • What the price includes.
  • Which surcharges apply.
  • Who prepares the AWB.
  • Who is responsible if the paperwork is rejected.
  • Which operator does the pickup and the delivery.
  • What requirements the goods must meet.

Ask for an itemised quote. That way you can compare options without mistaking a basic freight rate for the total door-to-door cost.

Air cargo security: the shipment must be protected

Air cargo is subject to specific security controls. It is not just a matter of checking that the package is well packed.

The security chain may include:

  • Access control at the premises.
  • Identification of the shipper.
  • Checking the goods.
  • Screening with security equipment.
  • Checking the paperwork.
  • Custody during preparation and transport.
  • Handover to a regulated agent or an approved haulier.

In Spain, AESA regulates and explains the air cargo security requirements, including the roles of the Regulated Agent, the Known Consignor and approved hauliers.

The aim is to keep the cargo protected from the moment it is prepared until it is handed over to the air operator.

If the goods do not meet the security requirements, they may face:

  • Extra inspections.
  • Delays in acceptance.
  • Flight changes.
  • Handling costs.
  • Rejection of the shipment.

Prepare the load in advance and confirm who keeps the security chain intact.

Dangerous goods: IATA DGR is not the same as ADR

Dangerous goods carried by air must comply with the IATA DGR (Dangerous Goods Regulations) and the technical instructions that apply to air transport.

The IATA DGR sets requirements for:

  • Classification of the goods.
  • UN number.
  • Proper shipping name.
  • Hazard class.
  • Packing group.
  • Packing instructions.
  • Maximum quantities.
  • Marking and labelling.
  • Documentation.
  • Restrictions for passenger aircraft or cargo-only aircraft.

Where the shipment requires it, you will also have to prepare the Shipper’s Declaration for Dangerous Goods.

Always check IATA's official information on dangerous goods and AESA's information on dangerous goods by air.

Where does ADR come in?

ADR governs the carriage of dangerous goods by road. The IATA DGR governs the air leg.

So if you run an operation from a warehouse in Spain to an airport and then send the goods by air, both frameworks may apply:

  • ADR: pickup and delivery by road.
  • IATA DGR: the air leg.
  • Customs rules: where there is export, import or international transit.

ADR does not replace the IATA DGR. Packaging that is valid for road may not be accepted on an aircraft.

Check the shipment for each mode of transport before loading it. For the road leg, also see the ADR 2025 framework published by UNECE.

Lead times and costs: fast, but with more variables

An urgent air shipment can move in 24 to 72 hours, although the total time depends on:

  • Pickup at origin.
  • Consolidation.
  • Flight availability.
  • Stopovers.
  • Security checks.
  • Customs.
  • Final delivery.
  • The type of service booked.

Don't confuse flight time with door-to-door lead time. The goods also have to be picked up, accepted, inspected, unloaded and delivered.

The price usually includes several items:

  • Air freight by chargeable weight.
  • Fuel surcharge.
  • Security.
  • Airport handling.
  • AWB issuance.
  • Customs clearance.
  • Storage.
  • Road delivery.
  • Special handling.
  • Dangerous goods surcharge.
  • Transport insurance.

Always ask for the total price and the expected delivery date. A cheap freight rate may not include the last mile, customs formalities or security surcharges.

Air freight within an intermodal chain

The aircraft rarely works alone. The usual approach is to combine several modes:

1. Pickup by road. 2. Preparation and consolidation at the terminal. 3. Air transport. 4. Import clearance. 5. Final delivery by road. This combination lets you use the road for flexibility and the aircraft for speed.

The paperwork must travel with the shipment all the way. If a document is missing at pickup or the details don't match the AWB, the problem can bring the whole chain to a halt.

Checklist before sending goods by air

Check these points:

  • Dimensions: work out the volumetric weight.
  • Weight: confirm the gross weight of each package.
  • AWB: check the shipper, consignee, route and goods.
  • Packaging: use packaging suited to airport handling.
  • Security: confirm the operator responsible.
  • Dangerous goods: check IATA DGR, and ADR if there is a road leg.
  • Customs: prepare the invoice, packing list and any documents required.
  • Lead time: separate flight time from door-to-door lead time.
  • Cost: insist on a quote with every surcharge included.
  • Records: keep all the paperwork in digital form.
