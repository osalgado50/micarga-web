The transport to come

The future of the electric truck in Spain: 24 charging points for heavy vehicles and just 0.5% of the market

The electric truck is no longer a distant promise. Manufacturers are presenting models capable of covering ever longer routes, transport companies are testing new operations and Europe is keeping demanding decarbonisation targets.

But Spain is moving far too slowly.

As of September 2026, there are only 24 charging points specifically adapted for heavy vehicles, compared with more than 42,000 points aimed mainly at light vehicles. On top of that, registrations of electric trucks over 16 tonnes barely account for 0.5% of the market and fell by 47% during the first half of 2026.

The technology is ready. The infrastructure is not yet.

The electric truck market is still at rock bottom

Today's picture is clear: Spanish heavy transport still depends almost entirely on diesel.

According to the figures published by ANFAC:

  • 0.5%: electrification share in trucks over 16 tonnes.
  • 47% fewer: electric truck registrations during the first half of 2026.
  • 1.6%: share of zero-emission industrial vehicles in Spain in 2025.
  • 15 years: approximate average age of the industrial vehicle fleet.
  • 2075: the year Spain would reach the targets set for 2030 if it keeps the current pace.

The 2075 figure works as a direct warning to manufacturers, administrations and transport companies. If fleet renewal does not speed up, Spain will lose competitiveness and find it harder to meet European targets.

Don't wait for the obligation to reach your fleet. Start analysing your routes, your costs and your documentation now.

Only 24 charging points are really suitable for trucks

Spain has thousands of public chargers. However, most of them are designed for cars and light commercial vehicles.

An electric truck needs much more than a conventional fast charging point:

  • High power: the batteries of a heavy vehicle require chargers of hundreds of kilowatts.
  • Enough space: the vehicle combination must be able to enter, manoeuvre and leave without difficulty.
  • Suitable access: the station must be close to logistics corridors and main roads.
  • Controlled time: charging has to fit in with the driver's mandatory breaks.
  • Real availability: it is not enough for the charger to appear on a map; it must be working and ready for trucks.

The gap between the network for light vehicles and the network for heavy vehicles is huge:

More than 42,000 points for light vehicles.

Only 24 points specifically adapted for heavy vehicles.

ANFAC is calling for Spain to reach 1,614 truck-specific points by 2035, with power ratings of 350, 800 and 1,200 kW. The leap required is obvious. European regulation also sets concrete targets. The AFIR Regulation establishes that, on the core trans-European network, charging points for heavy vehicles must progressively be placed at most 60 kilometres apart and offer high power ratings.

Check the requirements of the European AFIR regulation and see how they may affect your international routes.

The upfront price is still the big obstacle

An electric truck costs more than an equivalent diesel model. The difference is mainly explained by the price of the battery, the energy management technology and still limited production.

For a small company or a self-employed haulier, the initial investment can be hard to take on. And the problem grows when you also need to install chargers at the depot, increase the electrical capacity or adapt the premises.

However, the purchase price is not the only figure you should weigh up.

A proper analysis must include the total cost of ownership:

  • Energy: electricity can work out cheaper than diesel when you charge at your depot on a suitable tariff.
  • Maintenance: the electric motor has fewer moving parts and cuts down certain mechanical jobs.
  • Urban access: some cities are extending restrictions on the most polluting vehicles.
  • Commercial image: more and more customers demand transport with a smaller environmental footprint.
  • Financing and grants: public programmes can reduce the investment when specific funding lines are opened.

The saving compared with diesel is not the same on every route. It depends on the price of electricity, daily kilometres, the load, the contracted power and the charging system. But in predictable operations with depot charging, the electric truck can offer a significant reduction in energy costs.

Run your numbers before you buy. Compare consumption, maintenance, financing and charging availability.

Spain has gone two years without specific grants

Since April 2024, the sector has been denouncing the lack of stable, specific programmes for acquiring zero-emission heavy industrial vehicles. This lack of support holds back purchasing decisions. A company may be interested in electrifying its fleet, but not willing to take the risk of a large investment without knowing the incentives, the timelines or the infrastructure available.

ANFAC is calling for:

  • Direct grants to buy electric trucks.
  • Tax incentives for companies and the self-employed.
  • Scrappage programmes to take old vehicles off the road.
  • Subsidies to install chargers at logistics depots.
  • Specific funding lines for heavy vehicle charging stations.

Meanwhile, the National Action Framework for alternative fuels, presented in September 2026, aims to consolidate the charging and alternative fuels network in Spain.

The plan includes strengthening public charging, promoting installations at private depots and paying attention to safe parking areas for trucks. It also foresees measures for certain service stations to add 400 kW of power and, later, up to 600 kW.

The direction is set. Now it has to be carried out.

There are already electric trucks covering 1,000 kilometres a day

The electric truck is not only for urban delivery. New models are extending their range and improving charging speed.

Manufacturers such as Volvo, Scania, MAN, Mercedes-Benz, DAF and other international players are presenting vehicles capable of covering long distances with proper planning. Some large electric trucks already cover up to 1,000 kilometres a day by combining:

  • Overnight charging at the depot.
  • Charging during mandatory breaks.
  • High-power chargers.
  • Optimised routes.
  • Smart consumption management.
  • Efficient driving and energy recovery.

Megawatt charging systems will make it possible to recover a large part of the battery during breaks of 30 to 45 minutes, as long as there is enough infrastructure.

So the technology can already deliver in certain operations. The challenge is choosing carefully where to use it.

Which kind of transport can be electrified first?

The transition will not be the same for every company. The first operations with electric trucks will be those that meet several criteria:

  • Known routes: repetitive journeys that are easy to plan.
  • Controlled mileage: daily distance compatible with the available range.
  • Return to base: the chance to charge every night.
  • Stable loads: predictable weights and transport conditions.
  • Access to energy: enough electrical capacity at the premises.
  • Committed customers: contracts that value emissions reduction.

Regional transport, food distribution, retail logistics, port services and some medium-distance routes can be good entry points.

For a long, variable international route, you may still need a mixed solution. Analyse each vehicle separately. Don't electrify because it's fashionable: electrify where the savings and the operation are guaranteed.

Digital documentation will be key in the new mobility

An electric truck needs more precise planning. You have to keep track of the route, the breaks, the load, consumption, the points available and any incidents.

You will also need to keep your transport documentation organised:

  • Consignment note.
  • Vehicle documentation.
  • Proofs of delivery.
  • Load records.
  • Route incidents.
  • Authorisations and permits.
  • Information for checks and inspections.

The future will depend on the National Action Framework

The National Action Framework presented in September 2026 could become a turning point. Its aim is to organise the roll-out of infrastructure and coordinate the needs of transport, energy and mobility.

For it to work, it must translate into concrete results:

  • More truck chargers on the main corridors.
  • Stations ready for long vehicle combinations.
  • Charging at secure parking areas.
  • Faster permits.
  • Specific grants for heavy vehicles.
  • A stronger electricity grid.
  • Public, reliable information on availability.

The difference between meeting the European targets or reaching them in 2075 will depend on how quickly these measures are carried out.

Conclusion: the electric truck will arrive, but it won't wait for anyone

The Spanish market starts from a difficult position:

24 charging points specifically for heavy vehicles.

0.5% share in trucks over 16 tonnes.

47% drop in registrations during the first half of 2026.

Two years without specific grants.

But there are also positive signs. Manufacturers already offer vehicles with more range, megawatt charging cuts waiting times and some electric trucks cover 1,000 kilometres a day with energy costs below diesel on certain routes. The future is not about replacing every truck at once. It is about planning each operation, choosing the right routes and keeping better control of costs.
