The transport to come

The future of road freight transport: digitalisation, rail and new energies

The future of road freight transport is already under way. It won't arrive all at once, but it will change the way you work from 2026 onwards.

The road will remain essential. However, it will be a more digital, more controlled road, with vehicles that gradually pollute less.

If you're a self-employed haulier or you run a fleet, you'll need to keep on top of four areas:

  • Digital paperwork.
  • Tachograph and regulatory compliance.
  • Rail and intermodality.
  • Electrification and alternative fuels.

Adapting doesn't have to be complicated. Start by digitalising your documents, putting your processes in order and getting to know the changes that directly affect your route.

NEW RULES: PAPER GIVES WAY TO THE DIGITAL

DOCUMENT

The most immediate change arrives on 5 October 2026.

Law 9/2025 on Sustainable Mobility establishes that the Documento de Control Administrativo must be digital ten months after the law comes into force.

In practice, the electronic DeCA must be available before the service begins and can be checked through a secure URL or QR code.

What does it mean for you?

  • Less paper: stop printing, filing and carrying physical documents.
  • Instant availability: show the document from your phone during a roadside check.
  • Traceability: keep the creation date, the changes and the signatures.
  • Fewer mistakes: avoid incomplete, illegible or out-of-date documents.
  • Digital compliance: get your business ready for a permanent transport compliance environment.

International transport is also moving towards digital transport documents. The e-CMR makes it possible to gradually replace the paper international consignment note with an electronic document offering traceability, digital signatures and shared access for shipper, haulier and consignee.

In 2026, the e-CMR is gaining ground and being built into more management systems. One point is worth clarifying: using the e-CMR does not mean that every international operation must already give up paper because of a general European Union obligation. Whether it can be used depends on the route, the countries involved and the applicable rules.

Even so, the direction is clear: fewer physical documents and more connected information.

RAIL WILL GROW, BUT THE ROAD WILL REMAIN

KEY

Europe wants to shift more freight from road to rail and other modes of transport. The 30% target comes up often in industry debates, but it's worth reading it correctly.

There is no general obligation requiring rail to carry 30% of all freight by 2030. That figure works as a strategic benchmark and a modal-shift target, especially for long journeys.

The European Commission proposes increasing rail freight traffic by 50% by 2030 compared with 2015 and doubling it by 2050.

Spain starts from a small rail share. Law 9/2025 itself puts it at roughly 4% of tonne-kilometres and sets out measures to boost rail freight.

The most frequently quoted national target for 2030 is to reach around 10%.

How will rail be boosted?

  • Rolling motorways: lorries or semi-trailers will be able to cover part of the journey on a train.
  • Discounts on track access charges: incentives on the cost of using rail infrastructure are planned for a minimum of five years.
  • Logistics hubs: there will be more terminals connecting road, rail, ports and warehouses.
  • Strategic corridors: the Mediterranean and Atlantic axes and the links with ports will become more important.
  • Intermodality: a single consignment will combine several modes with closer digital coordination.

The road isn't going away. Its role will change. Rail will be more competitive on long journeys and large volumes. The lorry will remain essential for collection, regional distribution, the last mile and the link with the end customer.

For you, this may mean working more often as part of a multimodal chain. Paperwork, schedules and deliveries will need to be perfectly coordinated.

MORE INFRASTRUCTURE, BUT ALSO TIGHTER CONTROL OF

COSTS

The future of road freight doesn't depend only on buying new lorries. It also requires improving existing infrastructure.

Public planning is moving towards:

  • Road maintenance: more upkeep and adaptation of the network.
  • Safe rest areas: especially necessary to comply with driving and rest times.
  • Logistics hubs: interchange points between road, rail and other modes.
  • Charging points: facilities suited to electric lorries.
  • Real-time information: data on traffic, parking, charging and restrictions.
  • Investment appraisal: new infrastructure will have to make a better case for its economic, social and environmental return.

Pressure on road costs will also rise. Low-emission zones, possible pay-per-use charging schemes, energy prices, tolls and environmental requirements will all affect how each operation is planned.

That's why you'll need to know the real cost of each route. Working out the fuel won't be enough. You'll also need to weigh up:

  • Driving time.
  • Tolls and restrictions.
  • Energy consumption.
  • Access to urban areas.
  • Loading or refuelling time.
  • Cost of waiting.
  • Paperwork and compliance.

The business that keeps the best control of this data will be able to fine-tune its prices and avoid losses.

ELECTRIC VEHICLES AND ALTERNATIVE FUELS

Electrification will advance first in urban and regional delivery. In these services, distances are more predictable and vehicles can be charged overnight or at logistics bases.

In long-distance heavy haulage, the transition will be more gradual. Several solutions will be combined:

  • Battery-electric lorries: especially suited to urban and regional distribution.
  • Biomethane and renewable fuels: useful for cutting emissions without replacing the whole fleet straight away.
  • Hydrogen: with potential for long routes, although it still depends on cost and the supply network.
  • Synthetic and advanced fuels: intended for operations where electrification is more complex.

The European AFIR Regulation requires Member States to roll out alternative charging and refuelling infrastructure.

Charging points specifically for heavy vehicles and hydrogen stations will gradually be installed along the trans-European TEN-T network. The aim is for you to be able to run international routes with less dependence on conventional diesel.

What should you do now?

Don't change your whole fleet without analysing your routes. Start by looking at:

  • Daily kilometres.
  • Usual load.
  • Gradients and type of road.
  • Time available for charging.
  • Location of your base.
  • Total cost per kilometre.
  • Public grants available.

Technology must adapt to your operation. Not the other way round.

THE TACHOGRAPH WILL REACH MORE VEHICLES

From 1 July 2026, light commercial vehicles of more than 2.5 tonnes and up to 3.5 tonnes used for international transport or cabotage for hire or reward must be fitted with a second-generation smart tachograph.

The obligation applies to vehicles registered in the European Union carrying out international freight transport or cabotage for payment.

This includes many vans and light vehicles that until now worked under a different level of control.

The practical impact will be immediate

  • More records: driving, breaks, border crossings and activity.
  • More inspections: the data can be checked at the roadside.
  • More planning: you'll have to organise your breaks before starting the route.
  • More integration: the tachograph will have to connect with fleet management.
  • More responsibility: poor planning can lead to delays or fines.

Always check the exemptions and the exact scope before operating. The European Commission explains the smart tachograph timetable.

HOW DOES THE FUTURE OF TRANSPORT AFFECT THE

SELF-EMPLOYED HAULIER?

For the self-employed haulier, the future doesn't just mean buying a new lorry. It means working with more information and less room to improvise.

You'll need to keep on top of five points:

1. Paperwork: generate the DeCA and the consignment note correctly before setting off. 2. Costs: work out the real price of each trip and each kilometre. 3. Technology: use simple apps that work from the cab. 4. Training: stay up to date on tachograph, ADR, load securing and European rules. 5. Energy: decide when and how to bring in low-emission vehicles. The upside is that many tasks that eat up time today can be automated. You can issue a document from your phone, keep it in the cloud, share it with the office and show it at a roadside check in seconds.

GET READY TODAY FOR TOMORROW'S TRANSPORT

The future of road freight transport will be:

  • Digital, because the electronic DeCA will be mandatory from 5 October 2026.
  • Intermodal, because road and rail will have to work together.
  • Controlled, because the tachograph will reach more vehicles.
  • Sustainable, because electrification and alternative fuels will grow.
  • Traceable, because every operation will leave more data behind.
  • Competitive, because you'll have to keep tighter control of your costs.
