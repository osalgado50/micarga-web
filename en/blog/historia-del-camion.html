History of transport

The history of the lorry: from the first load-carrying engine to the connected truck

The lorry was not born the way you know it today. It had no insulated cab, no automated gearbox, no GPS and no driver aids. Nor could it cover hundreds of kilometres without stopping.

And yet, from the start it solved the same problem that still matters in transport: moving goods faster, more flexibly and more efficiently.

The history of the lorry is also the history of how the haulier's trade has changed. Every advance cut times, raised the payload and gave more control over the route.

This is the journey from the origins of the lorry to today's connected, electric vehicle.

From the animal cart to the first self-propelled load carrier

For centuries, moving goods depended on carts pulled by horses, oxen or other animals. Capacity was limited by the strength available, the state of the track and the distance that could be covered in a day.

The first great change came with steam.

In 1769 the French engineer Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot built the Fardier à vapeur, a self-propelled vehicle designed to haul artillery pieces. It was heavy, slow and hard to handle, but it introduced a decisive idea: a machine could move a load without depending on animals.

It was not yet a modern lorry. But it was one of its direct ancestors.

1881: the first semi-trailer appears

Even before the combustion engine spread, the idea of an articulated combination was already taking shape. In 1881, De Dion-Bouton developed a semi-trailer towed by a steam tractor. This solution foreshadowed one of the most important configurations in the history of transport:

  • Tractor unit: provides the traction.
  • Semi-trailer: carries the goods.
  • Coupling: allows the body or load unit to be swapped with more flexibility.

The practical consequence was clear: the vehicle could adapt better to different kinds of goods and operations.

1895-1896: the origin of the modern lorry

The real leap came with the internal combustion engine.

In 1895, Karl Benz presented a goods vehicle fitted with a combustion engine. A year later, in 1896, Gottlieb Daimler built what is usually considered the first modern commercial lorry: the Daimler Motor-Lastwagen.

Its figures look small next to today's vehicles:

  • Engine: two-cylinder Phoenix.
  • Power: around 4 hp.
  • Payload: 1,500 kg.
  • Top speed: around 12 km/h.

The engine sat at the rear and sent its power to the axle through a belt. According to Daimler Truck's corporate history, one of these first units made it to London.

Read Daimler Truck's official history of the first lorry for the technical details of this milestone.

That vehicle changed the trade because it made it possible to move goods without animal traction. It also forced new routines into being: engine maintenance, refuelling, breakdown repairs and route planning.

The haulier stopped being only an animal driver and became the operator of an industrial machine.

The First World War speeds things up

The First World War drove the development of heavy vehicles. Armies needed to move food, ammunition, fuel, parts and personnel without interruption.

The lorry then showed a decisive advantage: it could keep a steadier flow of supplies than traditional carts and depended less on the availability of animals. The war accelerated several advances:

  • More strength: chassis and suspensions built for harder loads.
  • More range: better tanks and engines.
  • Standardisation: more uniform parts and configurations.
  • Logistics capacity: moving large volumes between depots, factories and fronts.

In 1915, MAN fitted a diesel engine to a lorry. That step was decisive. Diesel offered better fuel consumption and high torque at low revs, two qualities that are especially useful for moving heavy weights.

In time, the diesel engine became the standard for heavy haulage.

The diesel engine turns the lorry into a professional tool

Over the following decades, the lorry stopped being an experiment and became part of the industrial economy.

Diesel made it possible to carry more over more kilometres. It also improved productivity on regional and long-distance routes.

For the haulier, the change was direct:

  • More load per trip.
  • Less need for frequent stops.
  • More regular deliveries.
  • The ability to work longer routes.
  • Lower operating cost per tonne carried.

Engine reliability started to matter as much as payload. A breakdown could hold up a delivery, delay a production line and cost several companies money.

The 1950s and 1960s: roads, semi-trailers and the cold chain

After the Second World War, industrial expansion and new road building transformed road haulage.

Through the 1950s and 1960s, the tractor-and-semi-trailer combination established itself as one of the main ways of moving goods. The reason was practical: it offered capacity, flexibility and speed when loading and unloading.

The development of semi-trailers also allowed transport to specialise:

  • Curtainsiders: general cargo.
  • Box bodies: goods protected from the weather.
  • Tankers: liquids and bulk products.
  • Flatbeds: machinery and special loads.
  • Refrigerated units: food and temperature-sensitive goods.

The cold chain changed food distribution in particular. Products could travel further while being kept in controlled conditions.

For the haulier this meant new responsibilities: controlling the temperature, meeting delivery times, checking the refrigeration units and keeping evidence of the job.

The lorry no longer just carried goods. In many cases it also had to guarantee the conditions they were kept in.

Electronics arrive in the cab

From the 1970s and 1980s, electronics were gradually built into the lorry. Engine management systems improved fuel consumption and gave finer control over how the vehicle ran. Then came electronic brakes, diagnostic systems and driver aids.

The trade changed again. The haulier needed to understand not only the mechanics but also the electronic warnings, the fault codes and the safety systems.

The cab became an information centre.

The tachograph also spread as a tool for recording speed, driving times, breaks and rest periods. Later, the digital tachograph replaced the paper discs with electronic records and driver cards.

Since 2006, new vehicles covered by European rules have carried a digital tachograph. Today the smart tachograph also allows tighter control of international operations.

The European Commission explains the rules and the evolution of the tachograph.

From GPS to the connected truck

Through the 1990s and 2000s, GPS and telecommunications changed how fleets are run.

Before, a company depended mainly on phone calls. Now it can know where the vehicle is, check the state of the route and receive operational information almost instantly.

The connected truck brings together more and more functions:

  • Geolocation: you know where the vehicle is.
  • Telematics: you get fuel and driving data.
  • Predictive maintenance: you spot possible problems before a breakdown.
  • Fleet management: you coordinate vehicles, loads and deliveries.
  • Real-time communication: fewer calls and less waiting.
  • Document integration: you tie the goods to their paperwork.

This change matters especially for owner-drivers and small firms. Connectivity lets you work with more control without building a complex back office.

The electric truck opens the next stage

The first electric goods vehicles appeared more than a century ago, but their batteries had little range and limited capacity. That is why the diesel engine dominated heavy haulage for decades.

Things started to change from the 2010s. Better batteries, emissions restrictions and the need to cut urban noise brought the electric truck back.

Today, electric trucks are making headway above all in:

  • Urban distribution.
  • Regional deliveries.
  • Night operations.
  • Municipal services.
  • Routes that return to a fixed base.

The main challenge is combining range, payload, price and infrastructure. According to the analyses published by ACEA on charging infrastructure, Europe will need a wide network of charging points for lorries during this decade.

The electric truck is not just a change of engine. It also demands planning the stops, managing the energy and adapting the routes with accurate data.
