CMR transport: what the international consignment note is and when it is compulsory

If you do international road haulage, there is one essential document you have to keep on top of: the CMR consignment note.

The CMR sets out the conditions of the job, identifies the parties and serves as evidence at an inspection, a delivery, a claim or a dispute over damage, loss or delay.

What is the CMR in transport?

The CMR is the Convention on the Contract for the International Carriage of Goods by Road. The same term is also used for the international consignment note that travels with the goods.

The CMR consignment note records the agreement between:

  • Consignor: hands over the goods for carriage.
  • Carrier: receives the load and undertakes to move it.
  • Consignee: receives the goods at the agreed place.

Its job is simple: to put on record what is being carried, who hands it over, who carries it, where it is picked up and where it is delivered.

The 1956 CMR Convention applies to the commercial carriage of goods by road when the place of origin and the place of destination are in two different countries, provided at least one of them is party to the Convention.

Read the text of the CMR Convention published in the official gazette to check its legal scope.

When is the CMR consignment note compulsory?

The CMR consignment note goes with international carriage of goods by road covered by the CMR Convention.

You must issue it and carry it when:

  • You cross a border: the place of loading and the intended place of delivery are in different countries.
  • You do international road haulage: the job falls within the CMR Convention.
  • You need to evidence the contract: the document shows the terms agreed.
  • You carry goods for hire and reward: the job has to reflect the relationship between consignor, carrier and consignee.
  • You may be stopped at a check: the paperwork has to be available while the job is running.

Legally, the contract of carriage does not disappear because there is no consignment note. In practice, though, travelling without a CMR leaves you with no clear documentary evidence and can cause trouble at an inspection, a delivery or a claim.

So the right decision is straightforward: always create the CMR consignment note before starting an international job.

Don't confuse the CMR with other documents either. International postal transport, household removals and funeral transport are treated separately and fall outside the ordinary scope of the CMR Convention.

What details must a CMR consignment note include?

Article 6 of the CMR Convention sets out the main information the consignment note must contain.

Before you set off, check these details:

Consignor's details

Include the full name or company name and the address of the sender.

Carrier's details

State who is doing the carriage and give their full details. If several carriers are involved, you have to reflect the job correctly.

Consignee's details

Write the name and address of the person or company that will receive the goods.

Place and date of loading

Record where and when the goods are collected. This information evidences the start of the job.

Place of delivery

State the intended destination with the fullest address possible. Avoid vague addresses that can cause doubt.

Description of the goods

Set out the nature of the load and its packaging. If it is dangerous goods, you must also include the corresponding ADR information.

Number of packages and marks

Note the units, references, marks and identifying codes of the goods.

Gross weight or quantity

Include the total weight or the quantity carried, as applicable.

Charges and conditions of carriage

Record the price of the carriage, any additional charges and, where applicable, customs duties.

Administrative instructions

Add the customs, insurance or other formalities needed to complete the job.

Carrier's reservations

If you see visible damage to the goods or the packaging, put your reservations on record. Signing without any note can complicate a later claim.

Create every document with complete, legible information. One wrong detail can mean phone calls, waiting at the delivery and arguments about who is responsible.

Who signs the CMR?

The signature confirms each party's involvement in the job. Normally:

1. The consignor signs at the start, when handing the goods to the carrier. 2. The carrier signs on collection, confirming receipt. 3. The consignee signs on delivery, evidencing receipt.

The consignee's signature must come with the date and, if anything has gone wrong, the corresponding reservations.

If the goods arrive damaged, short or late, do not close the job without recording it on the document. Set out the problem and ask the consignee to sign.

What is the CMR consignment note for?

The CMR consignment note is not just a form. It is operational and legal evidence.

Evidence of the contract

It shows that there is a contract of carriage between the consignor and the carrier.

Control of the goods

It allows you to identify what was collected, how much of it and how it was packed.

Proof of delivery

The consignee's signature confirms the goods reached the stated place.

Allocation of responsibility

It helps establish who was supposed to do what, and under what conditions.

Handling claims

If there is a loss, damage or a delay, the CMR provides information to work out what happened.

Answering an inspection

At a check, you can show the document that evidences the job and its main terms.

The consignment note does not remove every responsibility in transport, but it does stop the job being left with no clear documentary reference.

CMR on paper or e-CMR: what changes?

The e-CMR is the electronic version of the CMR consignment note.

The 2008 additional Protocol on the electronic consignment note, in force in Spain, recognises that the electronic document and the paper document have the same legal force when its requirements are met.

With the e-CMR you can:

  • Create the job from your phone: no forms to print.
  • Share the information instantly: with the office, the consignor and the consignee.
  • Sign digitally: right on the screen.
  • Cut mistakes: by reusing saved details.
  • Check the document: at any point in the route.
  • Keep a copy: for claims or audits.
  • Avoid losing paper: everything is filed.

The e-CMR must keep the data intact, available and traceable. It must also let the parties and the authorities get at the information when they need to.

Using the electronic consignment note requires the parties to accept the system and the job to fall within the relevant scope.

CMR and DeCA: they are not the same document

This difference is fundamental to working properly in Spain.

  • CMR or e-CMR: it evidences the international road transport contract.
  • DeCA: it meets the Spanish administrative control obligation.
  • National consignment note: it documents the contract of carriage within Spain.

The CMR has to do with the international contract and with the responsibilities of the parties. The DeCA answers a Spanish administrative obligation.

So in certain international jobs with their origin, destination or a leg in Spain, both documents can exist side by side:

  • CMR or e-CMR to evidence the international contract.
  • DeCA to meet the administrative control required in Spain.

Do not swap one for the other automatically. Check what paperwork applies to your job and create each document where it belongs.

From 5 October 2026, the electronic administrative control document will be compulsory for the transport covered by the Spanish rules. Adapting is straightforward if you start now.

Checklist before starting an international job

Before leaving the depot or the warehouse, confirm:

  • Origin and destination: are they spelled correctly?
  • Parties involved: are the names and addresses complete?
  • Goods: does the description match the actual load?
  • Packages and weight: are the quantities right?
  • Vehicle: have you recorded the registration where it is needed?
  • Additional documents: are the invoices, customs papers or certificates included?
  • ADR: have you added the dangerous goods information?
  • Signatures: have the relevant parties signed?
  • Copies: can you show the document during the journey?

Do this check before you set off. It will take you less time than sorting out a problem on the road.
