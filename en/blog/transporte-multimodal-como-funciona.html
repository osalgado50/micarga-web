International and multimodal

Multimodal transport: how it works, what documents it needs and why it is the future

Multimodal transport combines several modes of transport to move goods from origin to destination: road, rail, sea or air.

The key is coordinating the whole journey under a single transport contract and one main document. That way you can move a load door to door with less paperwork, greater traceability and simpler control of each stage.

In 2026, digitalisation also stops being an optional advantage. The electronic control document will be mandatory in Spain from 5 October 2026 for national public road haulage. Get ready now and avoid duplication, lost documents and problems during an inspection.

What is multimodal transport?

Multimodal transport uses two or more modes of transport within the same logistics operation.

For example:

1. A truck collects the goods at a factory in Valencia. 2. The load travels by train to a port terminal. 3. A ship carries it to Italy. 4. Another truck makes the final delivery.

The goods stay inside a load unit, usually a container, a swap body or a semi-trailer. The contents do not need to be handled at each change of vehicle.

Multimodal transport versus intermodal transport

Both systems combine different modes, but there is an important difference:

  • Multimodal transport: usually organised under a single contract and one main document for the whole journey.
  • Intermodal transport: may use several independent contracts and documents, one for each leg or mode of transport.

In practice, multimodal simplifies management because the operator coordinates the entire operation. You do not have to negotiate each stage separately or chase documents from different providers.

One contract. One overall view. Fewer complications.

How does a multimodal operation work?

The process starts when the shipper defines the origin, the destination, the goods and the delivery requirements.

The multimodal operator then designs the most efficient combination:

  • Road: flexibility and door-to-door collection.
  • Rail: capacity for long distances and less dependence on the road.
  • Sea: large volumes and connections between continents.
  • Air: speed for urgent or high-value goods.

The operator books the different services, coordinates the transhipments and keeps the information centralised.

The load unit can move from a truck to a train or a ship without being opened. This reduces:

  • Handling.
  • Risk of damage.
  • Theft and losses.
  • Waiting times.
  • Errors in identifying the goods.

The load unit: the element that connects all modes

The load unit lets the goods travel continuously, even when the vehicle changes.

The most common ones are:

  • Container: used especially in sea, rail and road transport.
  • Swap body: an interchangeable structure designed to make transfers between truck and train easier.
  • Semi-trailer: can be carried on certain rail or sea services.
  • Consolidated pallet: useful in lower-volume combined operations.

Each unit must be correctly identified. The container number, the seal, the shipment code and the details of the goods must match across all documents.

What documents does multimodal transport need?

The paperwork depends on the countries, the modes used, the type of goods and the terms of the contract. However, there are several common documents.

FIATA FBL: the main multimodal document

The FIATA Multimodal Transport Bill of Lading, known as the FIATA FBL, is a document created for multimodal operations organised by freight forwarders.

It can cover the whole journey under a single document. FIATA also provides for the digital FBL and the FWB, a non-negotiable multimodal transport document.

Check FIATA's official documents for their templates and conditions of use.

The FBL may include:

  • Shipper and consignee details.
  • Origin and destination.
  • Description of the goods.
  • Load unit and seal number.
  • Planned means of transport.
  • Delivery conditions.
  • Loading instructions.
  • Information on freight charges and liabilities.

e-CMR on the road legs

When a stage is carried out by road, the e-CMR can be used: the electronic version of the international consignment note governed by the CMR Convention.

In 2026, digital documentation is gaining ground for two reasons:

  • The DeCA will be mandatory in Spain from 5 October 2026 for national public road haulage.
  • The European Union is moving towards accepting electronic information through the eFTI framework.

Important: the e-CMR does not automatically become mandatory for all international transport in Spain. Whether it applies depends on the scope of the operation and the countries involved. Even so, it is a practical way to cut paper, improve proof of delivery and centralise information.

For national transport subject to the DeCA, the document must exist in electronic form before the service starts. It can include a QR code or a system that allows it to be consulted during a check.

Additional documents

Depending on the operation, you may also need:

  • Commercial invoice.
  • Load list or packing list.
  • Customs documentation.
  • Certificates of origin.
  • Dangerous goods documents.
  • Insurance policy or certificate.
  • Special authorisations.
  • Proof of delivery.

Do not keep these documents in scattered chats or folders that are impossible to find. Centralise them and access them directly from your phone or computer.

Who is liable in multimodal transport?

The multimodal operator coordinates the different legs and, when it issues a document such as the FBL, may take on the role of contractual carrier towards the shipper.

This means it must organise the transport from origin to destination in line with the agreed terms. However, the specific liability may depend on the leg on which the damage or loss occurs.

Different regimes may apply:

  • Road: CMR Convention.
  • Sea: maritime rules and conditions applicable to the contract.
  • Rail: specific rail transport regulations.
  • Air: the relevant international convention.

Digitalisation does not change liability limits on its own. What it does improve is the evidence:

  • Who received the goods.
  • When a transhipment took place.
  • What condition the load unit was in.
  • Who recorded an incident.
  • When the delivery was signed.

Full traceability reduces disputes and speeds up claims handling.

Traceability: track every movement in real time

Traceability is one of the great benefits of digitalised multimodal transport.

Each shipment can be linked to:

  • A unique identifier.
  • A container or swap body.
  • A seal number.
  • A FIATA document.
  • An e-CMR for the road leg.
  • A QR code.
  • Loading, unloading and transhipment records.

With this information, you can tell whether the goods are:

  • At the origin terminal.
  • In rail transit.
  • At the port.
  • On board the ship.
  • At the destination terminal.
  • Out for final delivery.

The goal is not to pile up more documents. It is to work with digital transport documents and logistics documentation that are connected, accessible and verifiable.

Multimodal transport and sustainability

Multimodality also helps build more sustainable freight transport.

Rail and sea can take on the longest stretches, while the road is used for collection and final delivery. This combination makes it possible to:

  • Cut road kilometres.
  • Optimise the capacity of each vehicle.
  • Lower fuel consumption.
  • Avoid part-empty trips.
  • Improve route planning.
  • Reduce emissions per unit transported.

It is not about eliminating road transport. It is about using each mode where it is most efficient.

A company that combines road, rail and sea properly can gain in cost, capacity, predictability and sustainability.

How to prepare for multimodal transport in 2026

Start with a clear document flow:

1. Identify the load unit. Record the container, swap body, semi-trailer and seal. 2. Define the main contract. Decide whether you will use an FBL, an FWB or another document. 3. Document each leg. Add an e-CMR where it applies to road transport. 4. Prepare the DeCA. From 5 October 2026, national transport subject to the obligation must have the electronic document. 5. Record the events. Loading, unloading, transhipment, incident and delivery. 6. Keep the evidence. Store documents, signatures, photos and receipts. 7. Train the team. The driver must know where to find the documentation during a check.

Frequently asked questions about multimodal transport

What is the difference between multimodal and intermodal transport?

Multimodal transport is usually organised with a single contract and one main document for the whole journey. Intermodal uses several modes of transport, but may work with separate contracts and documents for each leg.

Is using the FIATA FBL mandatory?

Not every operation is required to use an FBL. It is a standard document that is especially useful for freight forwarders and operators coordinating multimodal transport under a single contract.

Will the e-CMR be mandatory in Spain in 2026?

The e-CMR is not generally mandatory for all international transport. In Spain, what will be mandatory from 5 October 2026 is the electronic control document for certain national public road haulage operations. The e-CMR can be used where appropriate and, if it contains the required information, can help meet the applicable documentation obligation.

What are the advantages of digitalising multimodal documentation?

You get immediate access, traceability, fewer errors, better coordination between operators and stronger evidence of every stage of the transport. You can also respond faster during an inspection or when an incident occurs.
