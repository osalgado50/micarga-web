History of transport

The history of transport in Spain: from the muleteer and the railway to the digital lorry

The history of transport in Spain is the history of how goods, people and the economy have moved.

For centuries, a load depended on the strength of a mule. Then came the railway. Later, the lorry turned the road into the country's great logistics artery. Today, transport is moving into a new stage: digital documents, traceability, connectivity and everything managed from a phone.

The industry has changed many times. But the goal has always been the same: to get goods from where they are to where they are needed, faster, more safely and better organised.

From dirt tracks to the first trade routes

Before engines, land transport depended on the tracks, the animals and the weather.

Muleteers were professionals who moved goods with mules, donkeys and horses. They formed pack trains and covered long distances between villages, markets, ports and cities.

They carried essentials such as:

  • Grain.
  • Wine and oil.
  • Wool and cloth.
  • Minerals.
  • Tools.
  • Food and craft goods.

Mules were particularly useful in Spain for their stamina and their ability to keep going on mountain tracks. In many areas the muleteer was the only link between an isolated village and the big trading centres.

For heavier loads there were also carts and ox-drawn wagons. Transport was slow and expensive. On top of that, the tracks suffered from poor upkeep, a lack of safety and no infrastructure.

Speed depended on the distance, the terrain and the animal. A breakdown was not solved by changing a wheel or calling out a recovery service: it could mean losing several days of the journey.

Even so, muleteering created the first professional freight networks in Spain. Their knowledge of the routes and the markets was indispensable for centuries.

The railway changes the rules in the 19th century

The arrival of the railway was one of the great turning points in the history of transport.

Trains could carry more, cover greater distances and hold a steadier speed than carts and pack trains. They also connected producing areas with ports, factories and cities.

On the mainland, the Barcelona–Mataró line, opened on 28 October 1848, is usually considered the first major Spanish passenger railway. Connections such as Madrid–Aranjuez followed soon after.

Earlier, in 1837, the Havana to Güines railway had opened, when Cuba was still part of the Spanish Crown. On the mainland there were also mining lines and railway projects that came before or ran alongside Barcelona–Mataró.

The spread of the railway brought deep changes:

  • More capacity: trains moved large volumes of goods.
  • Less dependence on the weather: routes became more predictable.
  • New markets: companies could sell further afield.
  • Industrial growth: coal, iron and farm produce circulated far more easily.
  • The decline of muleteering: the traditional trades lost their place on the main corridors.

The railway did not wipe out the muleteers overnight. For decades the two systems lived side by side. Animals and carts took goods to the stations, while the train covered the long hauls.

The motor vehicle and the birth of road haulage

The first motor vehicles appeared in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. They arrived gradually, but they changed logistics completely.

The lorry had one decisive advantage over the railway: it could do door to door. It did not depend on a particular station, and it did not require the load to be moved between different modes.

From the first decades of the 20th century, lorries and buses slowly replaced carts and stagecoaches. As the internal combustion engine became widespread, animal haulage came to an end in professional transport.

The new model made it possible to:

  • Pick the goods up at the factory itself.
  • Deliver them to the customer's warehouse.
  • Adapt the route to each job.
  • Reach villages with no rail connection.
  • Cut delivery times.

The road thus became a basic piece of economic infrastructure. Hauliers stopped depending solely on train timetables and started running more flexible routes.

From national roads to the motorway network

For much of the 20th century, freight ran on national roads, through towns, and along single-carriageway routes.

Industrial growth, rising consumption and the expansion of trade demanded better roads. From the second half of the 20th century, toll motorways were built and, later, high-capacity dual carriageways.

The motorway network connected Spain's main logistics hubs:

  • Madrid and the centre of the country.
  • Barcelona and the Mediterranean corridor.
  • Valencia and the Mediterranean ports.
  • Bilbao and the industrial north.
  • Seville and the south.
  • Galicia, Castile and León and the corridors towards Portugal.

The lorry became the main way of moving a large share of freight. Its flexibility mattered especially for full loads, regional distribution and jobs with several drops.

But the growth brought new problems too: congestion, accidents, emissions, driver rest, checking hours, and the need to run haulage firms professionally.

Modern regulation: the LOTT and the European Union

As professional haulage grew, a common legal framework became necessary. In Spain the basic reference is Act 16/1987 on Land Transport, known as the LOTT. It governs the essentials of road and rail transport, such as:

  • Entry into the haulage profession.
  • Transport authorisations.
  • How services are contracted.
  • What companies are responsible for.
  • Inspections and penalties.
  • How land transport is organised.

The LOTT is developed through the ROTT, approved by Royal Decree 1211/1990. You can also read the consolidated text of the LOTT in the official gazette.

Spain joining the European Economic Community in 1986 and the creation of the European single market in 1993 widened the field for Spanish firms even further.

Since then, hauliers have worked in a market with common rules on:

  • Access to the international market.
  • Driving and rest times.
  • Tachograph.
  • Road safety.
  • Emissions and the environment.
  • Competition and working conditions.
  • Transport paperwork.

The route no longer ends at the border. A Spanish company can work day in, day out with customers, suppliers and hauliers across Europe.

From paper to the e-CMR

For decades the paperwork travelled in folders, envelopes and filing cabinets. Delivery notes, consignment notes, control documents and receipts went along physically with the goods.

Digitisation has changed that.

In international transport, the e-CMR allows the consignment note set out in the CMR Convention to be handled electronically. The additional Protocol recognises that an electronic consignment note can have the same legal force as the paper version when it meets the requirements laid down.

You can read the Protocol on the electronic consignment note.

The e-CMR makes it easier to:

  • Share the paperwork instantly.
  • Record electronic signatures.
  • Cut copying mistakes.
  • Check the status of a job.
  • Avoid losing documents.
  • Feed the information into management systems.

In Spain, the next step is the electronic DeCA, the Administrative Control Document. According to the published timetable, from 5 October 2026 the administrative control document must be handled electronically for the services covered by the obligation.

The Resolution of 5 June 2026 sets technical requirements for the digital document: how it must be available, the QR code, traceability and how long it must be kept.

The digital lorry is already on the road

Today's lorry does not only carry goods. It carries data too.

The driver uses a phone to check routes, talk to the office, sign documents and produce the details of the job at a roadside check. The company needs to know which job was done, who signed it and where each document is.

This change does not replace the haulier's experience. It backs it up.

A history of constant adaptation

The history of transport in Spain shows an industry that has never stopped adapting.

The muleteer had to know the tracks. The railwayman learned to work with timetables and stations. The professional lorry driver took on engines, tachographs, GPS and new rules. Now the haulier also has to handle electronic documents, QR codes and traceability systems.

The change is not simply swapping paper for a screen. It is working with more control, fewer mistakes and paperwork that is available at any moment.

For centuries, moving goods meant trusting the animal, the track and your memory. Today you can control the job from your phone and prove every step with a digital document.

That is where transport is now. And it is also its next stage.
