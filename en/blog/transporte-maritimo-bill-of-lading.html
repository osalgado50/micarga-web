International and multimodal

Sea freight transport: the bill of lading and everything you need to document

Sea freight moves large volumes between continents, but it also demands documentary precision. A mistake in the bill of lading, an incorrect weight on the packing list or a misread Incoterm can cause delays, extra costs and customs problems.

The fix is simple: prepare every document before the cargo reaches the port and keep all the information available to your team, your freight forwarder and your carrier.

Bill of lading: the core document of sea freight

The bill of lading, also known as B/L, is the main document of a sea freight operation.

It is issued by the shipping line, the ocean carrier or the relevant agent. It serves several purposes:

  • Contract of carriage: it sets out the terms of service between the shipper and the shipping line.
  • Receipt for the goods: it confirms that the cargo has been received.
  • Proof of shipment: it certifies that the goods have been loaded or accepted for carriage.
  • Control document: it identifies the vessel, origin, destination, consignee, packages and type of goods.
  • Document of title: in certain formats, it allows rights over the cargo to be transferred.

Always check that the B/L matches the commercial invoice and the packing list. Pay particular attention to:

  • Shipper's name and address.
  • Consignee and notify party.
  • Port of loading and port of discharge.
  • Description of the goods.
  • Number of packages.
  • Gross weight and volume.
  • Container and seal number.
  • Agreed Incoterm.
  • Shipment date.

One wrong detail can hold up delivery or require a documentary correction with costs and delays. See also Maersk's practical explanation of the bill of lading.

Types of bill of lading you should know

Not all bills of lading have the same consequences. Before accepting the document, identify which type you need.

Clean bill of lading

A clean B/L states that the goods were received in apparent good order and that there are no visible remarks about damage, defective packaging or shortages.

It is common when banks, letters of credit or buyers requiring clear proof of the cargo's condition are involved.

On-board bill of lading

An on-board B/L confirms that the goods have been physically loaded on board the vessel.

The shipment date can be decisive for meeting a contract, a delivery deadline or a banking condition. Don't confuse this document with a “received for shipment” B/L, which only certifies receipt for carriage, not necessarily loading on board.

Straight bill of lading

A straight B/L is issued to a named consignee and is non-negotiable. The shipping line delivers the goods to the stated consignee.

It is practical when the transaction has already been paid for or there is a stable business relationship between buyer and seller.

Order bill of lading

An order B/L is negotiable. It can be transferred by endorsement and is usually used in financed transactions or when payment is still pending.

Whoever lawfully holds the document can prove the right to receive the goods.

Sea waybill

A sea waybill works as a contract of carriage and a receipt, but it is not a negotiable document of title. The cargo is delivered to the named consignee without the need to present physical originals.

It is faster, but it requires trust between the parties. Choose this format only when the commercial risk is under control.

Packing list: detail exactly what travels inside the container

The packing list lets anyone identify the goods without opening every package. It is essential for the shipping line, the freight forwarder, customs, the warehouse and the carrier handling final delivery.

Include at least:

  • Shipper and consignee: who sends and who receives.
  • Number of packages: boxes, pallets, drums or units.
  • Description: detail the contents of each package.
  • Net and gross weight: avoid discrepancies during inspection.
  • Dimensions and volume: needed to plan the load.
  • Marks and numbers: make identification easier.
  • Invoice number: links the packing list to the commercial transaction.
  • Container and seal: essential for FCL shipments.

Don't replace the commercial invoice with the packing list. The invoice proves the value of the transaction; the packing list describes how the cargo is physically organised.

Customs declaration: prepare clearance without hold-ups

The customs declaration identifies the goods and determines the applicable regime: export, import, transit, warehousing or another procedure.

To prepare it, the customs agent usually needs:

  • Commercial invoice.
  • Packing list.
  • Bill of lading or sea waybill.
  • HS or TARIC tariff code.
  • Country of origin.
  • Value of the goods.
  • Incoterm.
  • Specific licences or authorisations.
  • Certificate of origin, where applicable.
  • Health, phytosanitary or technical documentation if the goods require it.

A generic description such as “spare parts” or “various products” may trigger an inspection. Describe the goods precisely and keep a digital copy of every document.

Incoterms: define who pays, who is liable and when the risk transfers

The International Chamber of Commerce's Incoterms® 2020 determine how costs, risks and responsibilities are shared between seller and buyer.

On their own, they do not govern ownership of the goods, nor do they replace the sales contract. Always state the full term and the agreed place.

In sea freight, the key ones are:

  • FOB: the seller delivers when the goods are on board at the port of origin. The buyer normally books the main freight.
  • CFR: the seller pays carriage to the port of destination, but the risk transfers at origin, when the goods are loaded.
  • CIF: the seller pays the freight and takes out minimum insurance to the port of destination. The risk also transfers when the cargo is on board at origin.
  • FAS: the seller delivers alongside the vessel at the port of loading.

For containers, check whether FCA better describes the actual point of delivery. In many operations the cargo is handed over to the terminal or the carrier before it goes on board. That detail can change how the risk is allocated.

FCL or LCL: choose the mode that best fits your cargo

FCL: Full Container Load

With FCL you use a full container for a single load or shipper.

Main advantages:

  • Less handling.
  • Greater control of the seal.
  • Lower exposure to damage from consolidation.
  • Usually more predictable lead times.
  • Best option for large volumes.

On the packing list, link each item to the corresponding container and seal number.

LCL: Less than Container Load

With LCL you share a container with goods from other shippers.

It suits small volumes because you pay for the space you use. However, the cargo goes through more consolidation and deconsolidation operations.

Practical consequences:

  • There may be more handling.
  • Lead times may be longer.
  • The risk of documentary issues increases.
  • You must carefully check the house B/L and the master B/L when a consolidator is involved.

Sea freight lead times and rates in 2026: plan with a buffer

In 2026, sea freight combines more moderate base rates with significant volatility. Vessel overcapacity is pushing prices down, but geopolitical conflicts, route diversions, congestion and peak season generate sharp spikes.

Some market references have estimated:

  • Average global freight rate corrections of roughly 17% to 25%.
  • Asia–Mediterranean spot rates close to 4,211 USD/FEU at certain points.
  • Later spikes of around 5,194 USD/FEU.
  • FAK rates that can range between 4,700 and 7,500 USD per FEU, depending on route, shipping line and date.

These figures are indicative. Always ask for an up-to-date quote and check what it includes:

  • Base freight.
  • Origin and destination THC.
  • Fuel surcharge.
  • Congestion.
  • Geopolitical risk.
  • Dangerous goods.
  • Demurrage and detention.
  • Inland delivery and customs clearance.

Lead times also need a buffer. 2026 reliability reports have put global vessel schedule reliability at roughly 56% to 62%, with average delays of around 5 or 6 days for services that arrive late. Build a buffer into your planning and don't commit to a final delivery based solely on the estimated time of arrival at port.

Decarbonisation: sea freight is changing too

Spain has approved a National Action Plan for the Decarbonisation of Maritime Transport with 250 million euros for the 2026-2030 period.

The funding is mainly aimed at:

  • Renewing and converting existing vessels.
  • Building low-emission vessels.
  • Developing renewable fuels such as methanol or ammonia.
  • Driving pilot projects.
  • Reducing emissions linked to maritime transport.

This plan is aimed mainly at shipping lines and fleet operators. It is different from the maritime eco-incentive, which seeks to shift freight from road to sea through short sea shipping services. See the details in the Ministry of Transport's official information on the maritime eco-incentive.

For you, the consequence is practical: documenting every shipment properly will matter more and more to prove volumes, routes, freight and modal shift.

Checklist before shipping

Before confirming departure, check:

  • Commercial invoice: correct value, currency, origin and Incoterm.
  • Packing list: packages, weights, dimensions and seals.
  • Bill of lading: consignee, ports, container and date.
  • Customs: TARIC code, permits and declared regime.
  • Insurance: cover taken out and certificate available.
  • FCL or LCL: the right mode and the right document.
  • Lead times: buffer added for delays.
  • Final delivery: carrier, address and documents available.
