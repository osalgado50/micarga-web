Dangerous goods

Vehicle, marking and ADR routes: what isn't paperwork but still gets checked

An ADR inspection doesn't stop at the paperwork. This is what gets looked at on the lorry, the load and the route.

Carrying dangerous goods takes more than having the documents in order. You also have to check the vehicle, the marking, the load securing and the route before every job. If you're after the document checklist (certificates, instructions in writing), it's in ADR paperwork: what the Guardia Civil asks for — here we cover everything else.

An approved vehicle

Certain tank journeys, battery vehicles, explosives and other specific configurations need an ADR approval certificate for the vehicle. Driving without one is among the breaches in risk category I, the most serious under the Resolution of 12 February 2026.

Visible marking

Before setting off, check:

  • Orange plates, front and rear (and on the sides if you're carrying in a tank).
  • Placards and labels on the packages.
  • That no marking from a previous load is left showing.
  • That the plates are clean, retroreflective and the required size.

Incorrect marking can prevent the hazard being identified in an accident and lead to the vehicle being held.

Equipment on board

Working fire extinguishers, chocks, hi-vis vest, torch, gloves, eye and respiratory protection where applicable, and material to contain small spills. Check it before every job, not once you're already at the check.

Load securing: the riskiest moment

The vehicle must be immobilised during loading and unloading. Before you start, check:

  • Packagings free of damage, leaks or deterioration, and correctly labelled.
  • Compatibility between packages: that there is no mixed-loading prohibition.
  • The load secured against movement and the weight within the maximum authorised mass.
  • The tank with the correct code, capacity, degree of filling and cleanliness.

When you finish, do a visual inspection: no spills, doors closed and marking matching the actual load.

ADR routes and the RIMP network: plan before setting off

Spain has compulsory itineraries for certain dangerous goods journeys: the Dangerous Goods Route Network (RIMP). There may also be time restrictions, bans, tunnel limits or a requirement to use specific motorways or ring roads.

Don't pick the route just because it's the shortest: an unauthorised route means an offence and a late delivery. The Mi Carga app includes planning and routes to help you prepare the journey from your phone.

What changes at 2026 inspections

The Resolution of 12 February 2026 groups breaches into three categories: I (high risk, immediate immobilisation), II (significant risk, to be corrected during the job) and III (low risk, corrected afterwards). An inspection can cover up to 27 points across documents, marking, equipment and the vehicle.
